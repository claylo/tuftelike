# The Opening Thread

Every book with something to say eventually has to decide what happens at
the edges of the page. Most choose to ignore the question entirely: the
margin is a buffer, a place for the eye to rest before the next line,
dead space bought and paid for by the paper merchant. This field guide
starts from a different premise. The margin is a second channel —
narrower than the main text, slower to fill, but never empty on
purpose.[^opening]

That premise sounds abstract until you watch someone read a page that
uses its margins well. Their eyes don't just travel left to right; they
drift sideways at the exact moment a claim needs a source, a term needs a
definition, or a tangent needs somewhere to live that isn't the middle of
a sentence. The main column keeps moving. The margin keeps up without
interrupting.

> A page with only one channel can only say one thing at a time. A page
> with two can argue with itself, footnote itself, and correct itself —
> all without breaking stride.

None of this requires exotic typesetting. It requires a decision, made
early, to treat the margin #index-main[margin] as addressable space rather than leftover
space. The chapters that follow work through what changes once you make
that decision — starting with the humblest unit of all: the single
numbered note, #index[margin note] anchored to one word, answerable in one breath.

[^opening]: This is the thesis of the whole book, if you want it in one
sentence: margins are read differently than body text, so they should be
written differently too — shorter, faster, more willing to digress.
