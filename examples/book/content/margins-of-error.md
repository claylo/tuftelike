# Margins of Error

Every measurement carries a margin of error — a band around the number
that says, honestly, how much to trust it. Pages could use the same
honesty. A paragraph that asserts something plainly, with no note in
sight, is claiming a confidence it may not have earned.[^confidence] The
margin is where that confidence gets checked.

This is not an argument for cluttering every page with caveats. It's an
argument for having somewhere to put them that isn't the main sentence.
Compare "the layout described in [the opening thread](#the-opening-thread)
works for most six-by-nine trade books" against the same claim followed
by a margin note listing the page-count ranges it was actually tested
against. The first sentence is confident. The second is honest, and no
slower to read, because the honesty lives one glance to the right.

### Quick Try: Margins

Take any paragraph you've already written and ask which sentences are
doing double duty — stating a claim *and* hedging it in the same breath.
Split the hedge into a margin note. Read the paragraph again. If it now
reads faster and asserts more clearly, the split was worth making.

Do this for a full page before deciding whether the technique earns a
permanent place in your draft. A single paragraph rarely tells you
enough; a page shows whether the margin column stays legible once
several notes compete for the same vertical space.

A worked exchange from a workshop transcript, kept verbatim because the
edit itself is the lesson:

<prompt>Split the hedges out of this sentence: "This approach probably
works for most six-by-nine books, though results may vary near the
page-count extremes."</prompt>

<response>Main sentence: "This approach works for six-by-nine books."
Margin note: "Tested across the 61–600 page range; extremes near either
end are the least verified."</response>

[^confidence]: Not a footnote about statistics — a footnote about tone.
"Probably" and "in most cases" are margin material; the main sentence
reads better without them.
